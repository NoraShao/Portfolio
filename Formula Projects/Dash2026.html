FSAE Dash

September 2025 - May 2026

The dash allows drivers to interact with the car while also displaying important telemetry on 7-segment displays in a sunlight-readable format.

For the 2026 season, the team decided to switch from a telemetry and driver input-integrated F1-style steering wheel (which I also worked on here) to a more traditional steering wheel with a separate dash that handles the telemetrydisplay and driver input. The dash is a standalone unit that displays telemetry and allows drivers to interact with the car, while the steering wheel is only used for steering. The modularity helped us save weight and made both devices more reliable.

The dash displays telemetry from the car's ECU sensors via 7-segment displays. They were chosen because drivers previously reported difficulty reading the screen on the steering wheel in bright sunlight, so we decided to use 7-segment displays for the dash because of their improved readability in bright sunlight. The dash displays telemetry such as speed, RPM, gear, coolant temperature, oil temperature, and battery voltage. It also allows drivers to control the car's electrical modules, such as the fans and water pump, by sending commands to the PDM.

The dash is connected to the car via a 6-pin quick-disconnect connector. It operates off 12V power from the car's electrical system. Most of the signals are transmitted to/received from the other car modules via CAN bus, but signals like the starter signal are directly wired. network.


Hardware Design

Schematic Design

Some of the features on the board include

  • MCU-to-connector SPI and I2C connections
  • USB-B connection to the USB (for future USB-firmware uploads in addition to the existing ST-LINK connection)
  • CAN transceiver for communication with the car's ECU and PDM
  • GPIO pins for additional functionality
  • Power regulators for the MCU
  • Power protection circuits for any input power

PCB Layout

The PCB for the dash is a 4-layer board with a ground plane and power plane; signal traces were routed on the top and bottom layers. The inner layers contain the ground and power planes.
The dash uses an STM32F405RGT microcontroller (which is the standard for most PCBs on the team).
I designed the PCB in Altium Designer following typical PCB design practices, such as:

  • Ensuring all ground nets were connected to an uninterrupted ground plane via vias ;
  • Using decoupling capacitors, particularly following the power scheme of the STM32F405 datasheet
  • Ensuring trace widths were appropriate for the current requirements
  • Placing MCU and high-speed peripherals on one side of the board, and noisy components like buck converters on the other side of the board
  • Routing differential pairs together
  • Following the manufacturer's recommended footprint for components—particularly for ICs such as the MCU and the power regulators



Firmware Design

I wrote the firmware for the board on STM32CubeIDE and VSCode. The firmware is written in C and uses the STM32 HAL library.
MAX7219 driver

The dash uses ProtoSupplies' 7-segment displays, which control the LED segments with a MAX7219 driver. I wrote a driver for the MAX7219 that handles the initialization of the MAX7219, and sends data to the displays via SPI. It also handles the multiplexing of the displays, so a single SPI connection can control multiple daisy-chained displays.
The driver is written in C and is designed to be used with the STM32 HAL library. It's independent and easily integrated into other projects.

WS2811 driver

I also found a separate, larger, single-digit 7-segment display intended for the gear positions display. It uses three daisy-chained WS2811s to control the LED segments. It was designed as a hobby project, so we had to order the display, the PCB, and the components separately. I wrote a driver for the WS2812 that handles the initialization of the WS2812, and sends data to the displays via a single GPIO pin, following the WS2812 datasheet. It also handles the multiplexing of the displays, so a single GPIO pin can control multiple daisy-chained displays.
Unfortunately, the display never ended up working, even though the signal input to the WS2812 was correct and verified with an oscilloscope, I just used an addition 5-digit ProtoSupplies display in the center of the dash for gear position instead.

RPM lights: I reused the RPM lights (visible in the image at the top of the page) from the previous steering wheel design design. The lights are controlled by the MCU via I2C buffers to a PCA9685 LED driver. The LEDs light up sequentially as the RPM increases, to indicate the driver when to shift gears.
CAN Communication: The dash is equipped with a CAN transceiver to facilitate communication with other vehicle systems. Since the dash's primary role is to display telemetry info from our ECU Haltech ELITE 1500, I just implemented the necessary CAN decoding protocol following this documentation.
